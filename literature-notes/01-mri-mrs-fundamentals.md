## 2. Concept Notes

### What does 7T mean?

7T refers to an ultra-high magnetic field strength of 7 Tesla. Higher magnetic field strengths generally improve signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and spectral resolution.

In magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), glutamate (Glu) and glutamine (Gln) produce overlapping resonance signals because of similarities in their molecular structures. Using a 7T scanner can improve researchers' ability to distinguish these spectral signals, allowing more reliable estimation of their respective concentrations.

Importantly, MRS measures metabolite signals rather than neuronal action potentials.

### What is the difference between structural MRI and MRS?

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) primarily provides anatomical information about the brain, allowing researchers to examine brain structures, tissue characteristics, and morphology.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), on the other hand, allows researchers to estimate concentrations of specific metabolites within selected brain regions.

Thus, structural MRI primarily addresses questions about brain anatomy, whereas MRS provides information about brain neurochemistry.

### Why distinguish glutamate (Glu) from glutamine (Gln)?

Glutamate is the brain's principal excitatory neurotransmitter, but it also plays important roles in cellular metabolism. Glutamine serves as a precursor for glutamate synthesis and participates in the glutamate–glutamine cycle between neurons and astrocytes.

Distinguishing these metabolites may provide additional insight into neurochemical differences associated with depression, stress, and treatment response.

Because Glu and Gln have overlapping spectral signals, researchers sometimes report their combined signal as Glx. However, a combined measurement may obscure differences between the two metabolites.

### What can MRS tell us about the brain, and what can it not tell us?

MRS allows researchers to estimate concentrations of metabolites in selected regions of the brain, including glutamate, glutamine, GABA, and N-acetylaspartate (NAA). These measurements can be compared across individuals, clinical populations, or experimental conditions.

However, elevated glutamate concentrations measured by conventional MRS do not necessarily indicate increased neuronal firing or excitotoxicity. The MRS glutamate signal is dominated by intracellular tissue pools, including glutamate involved in cellular metabolism.

Conventional MRS cannot directly distinguish glutamate released into synapses from intracellular glutamate or determine the rate of metabolic processes.

Therefore, elevated glutamate concentrations alone cannot establish increased excitatory neurotransmission, neuronal damage, or causation.
